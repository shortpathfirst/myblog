## Waves
A wave describes how something varies from place to place and how that pattern evolves over time.
>A wave is a pattern of oscillation that varies across space and time

A wave is a generalization of the harmonic oscillator allowing the oscillation to depend on **position and time** in other words is *a pattern of oscillations distributed through space and evolving in time*

$$
y(x,t)=A\cos(kx-\omega t).
$$
<sup>Harmonic wave dependant to position and time</sup>

if you choose one fixed position you obtain is a **harmonic oscillator in time**.So you can think of the wave as a spatially distributed collection of harmonic oscillations, with different phases at different positions.

### **Quantum mechanics waves**
In quantum mechanics, a wave function (or wavefunction) is a mathematical description of the quantum state of an isolated quantum system. 
Wave functions are complex-valued, so only its relative phase and relative magnitude can be measured. For quantum waves we can measure quantum state or probability amplitude

#### What is a eigenmode?
>An eigenmode is a natural, self-consistent pattern of oscillation of a system. once excited it oscillates with its own characteristic frequency while keeping overall shape.


A vibrating string has many possible shapes, but only certain special standing-wave patterns reproduce themselves as they oscillate.
**Each of these natural patterns is an eigenmode.**

# Part II — Introduce the quantum physics idea
Classical mechanics is insufficient at explaining microscopic phenomena because it assumes that particles have definite positions and trajectories.
At atomic scales experiments reveal that matter exhibits wave behaviour and particle behaviours. 
- Louis de Broglie predicted the wave nature of matter theoretically and won the 1929 Nobel Prize for it.
- J. J. Thomson discovered the electron as a particle, while his son G. P. Thomson later demonstrated the electron's wave nature experimentally.
  
Moreover, the **Heisenberg uncertainty principle** say that we cannot know position and momentum at the same time.
These phenomena cannot be fully described by classical mechanics, motivating the development of quantum mechanics.

This make us ask an important question:
>**If an electron behaves like a wave, what equation describes that wave?**

### Schrödinger equation
Conceptually, the Schrödinger equation is the quantum counterpart of Newton's second law in classical mechanics. 

Given a set of known initial conditions, **Newton's second law** makes a mathematical prediction as to what path a given physical system will take over time. The Schrödinger equation gives the evolution over time of the wave function, the quantum-mechanical characterization of an isolated physical system.

# Part III — Wave function

## Overview

The **wave function** $\Psi(\mathbf{r}, t)$ is the central object of quantum mechanics. It encodes the complete information about a quantum system.

$\psi$ is not directly interpreted as matter density.
Instead $|\psi(x,t)|^2 $ represents probability density.

- $|\Psi(\mathbf{r}, t)|^2$ is the **probability density** — the chance of finding the particle in a small volume around $\mathbf{r}$ at time $t$.
  
Because the particle must be somewhere in space we need a physically valid wavefunction.
- The wave function must be **normalised**: 
$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\psi(x,t)|^2dx=1.
$$

- For a particle in a time-independent potential, the solutions split into **stationary states** (energy eigenstates) whose time dependence is a pure phase factor $e^{-iE_n t/\hbar}$.

---

### Quantum numbers

For the hydrogen atom, each stationary state is labelled by three integers:

| Symbol | Name | Range | Physical meaning |
|--------|------|-------|------------------|
| $n$ | Principal | $1, 2, 3, \ldots$ | Energy level; larger $n$  higher energy, larger orbital |
| $l$ | Azimuthal (orbital) | $0 \le l < n$ | Magnitude of orbital angular momentum $L = \hbar\sqrt{l(l+1)}$ |
| $m$ | Magnetic | $-l \le m \le l$ | $z$-component of angular momentum $L_z = m\hbar$ |

The letters s, p, d, f correspond to $l = 0, 1, 2, 3$. The number of radial nodes (concentric shells of zero density) equals $n - l - 1$, and the number of angular nodes equals $l$.
The magnetic number explain that these different solutions describe different angular states/orientations.

## Part IV — Introduce the Schrödinger equation

$${\displaystyle i\hbar {\frac {d}{dt}}\vert \Psi (t)\rangle ={\hat {H}}\vert \Psi (t)\rangle }$$

$\Psi$ is the **quantum state** of the system, (m) is non-relativistic particle mass, (V) describes potential energy and

$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2
$$

represents kinetic energy operator.

We used to define the equation in **compact form** using the Hamiltonian:

$$
\hat H=
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2+V.
$$
>The Hamiltonian determines how the quantum state evolves in time.

The Hamiltonian is the quantum-mechanical counterpart of the total energy of a classical system. For a particle in a potential $E=T+V$, in quantum mechanics, position and momentum become operators.

#### The full equation
$$
i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}=
\left[
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2
+
V
\right]\Psi = \hat H\Psi.
$$




### Eigenstates

As discussed earlier for oscillating systems, **eigenstates play the role of natural modes of oscillation**. Each energy eigenstate evolves with a characteristic angular frequency determined by its eigenvalue

$$\omega_n=\frac{E_n}{\hbar}.$$


For energy eigenstate,
$$\hat H\psi_n=E_n\psi_n.$$

Therefore:

* $\psi_n$ = allowed quantum state, is an **energy eigenstate**, an allowed stationary mode of the system
* $E_n$ = is the **energy eigenvalue** associated with that state.
  
For the Schrödinger equation, the Hamiltonian ($\hat H$) has two central roles:
- **Energy**: Its eigenvalues give the possible energies of the system, the energy eigenstates are the natural modes of quantum evolution, each with it's own characteristic frequency.
- **Time evolution**:The Hamiltonian determines how the quantum state evolves in time in the Schrodinger equation.

# Part VII — Hydrogen Orbitals Visualization

### Hydrogen Atom Wavefunctions
The wavefunction for a hydrogen atom in spherical coordinates is:

$$\psi_{n,l,m}(r, \theta, \phi) = R_{n,l}(r) \cdot Y_l^m(\theta, \phi)$$

Where:
- $n$ = principal quantum number (1, 2, 3, 4, ...)
- $l$ = angular momentum quantum number (0 ≤ l < n)
- $m$ = magnetic quantum number (-l ≤ m ≤ l)
- $R_{n,l}(r)$ = radial wavefunction
- $Y_l^m(\theta, \phi)$ = spherical harmonics

The spherical harmonics determine the angular structure of orbitals.
### Radial Wavefunctions
The radial wavefunctions are:

$$R_{n,l}(r) = \sqrt{\left(\frac{2}{n a_0}\right)^3 \frac{(n-l-1)!}{2n(n+l)!}} e^{-\rho/2} \rho^l L_{n-l-1}^{2l+1}(\rho)$$

Where:
- $a_0$ = Bohr radius (≈ 0.529 Å, set to 1 in atomic units)
- $\rho = \frac{2r}{n a_0}$ = dimensionless radius
- $L_{n-l-1}^{2l+1}(\rho)$ = Associated Laguerre polynomial

---
#### Another way to write it

$$
R_{nl}(r)=
\left(\frac{2}{na}\right)^2
\sqrt{
\frac{a}{4}
\frac{(n-l-1)!}{[(n+1)!]^3}
}
\cdot
F_{nl}\left(\frac{2r}{na}\right)
$$

with

$$
F_{nl}(x)=x^l e^{-x/2}(n+1)!L_{n-l-1}^{2l+1}(x).
$$

For the **2D plot**, you set (y=0), so

$$
r=\sqrt{x^2+z^2},

\theta=\cos^{-1}\left(\frac{z}{r}\right),

\phi=\operatorname{atan2}(0,x).
$$

Therefore, the plotted function is simply

$$

f(x,z)
= R_{nl}!\left(\sqrt{x^2+z^2}\right)
\left|
Y_l^m\left(
\cos^{-1}\frac{z}{\sqrt{x^2+z^2}},
\operatorname{atan2}(0,x)
\right)
\right|

$$

with (a=1) $|\Psi| = |R\cdot Y|$ **not** $|\Psi|^2$  which would be the probability density.

### Angular nodes
For example, (s) states have no angular node, (p) states have one, and (d) states have two.


### Common Orbitals (in Cartesian coordinates)

#### 1s orbital
$$\psi_{1s}(x,y,z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}} \cdot 2 \cdot e^{-2r}$$
where $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}$

#### 2s orbital
$$\psi_{2s}(x,y,z) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{2\pi}} \left(2 - r\right) e^{-r/2}$$

#### 2p orbitals
$$\psi_{2p_x}(x,y,z) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{2\pi}} \cdot x \cdot e^{-r/2}$$
$$\psi_{2p_y}(x,y,z) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{2\pi}} \cdot y \cdot e^{-r/2}$$
$$\psi_{2p_z}(x,y,z) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{2\pi}} \cdot z \cdot e^{-r/2}$$

#### 3s orbital
$$\psi_{3s}(x,y,z) = \frac{1}{9\sqrt{3\pi}} \left(6 - 6r + r^2\right) e^{-r/3}$$

#### 3d orbitals (examples)
$$\psi_{3d_{z^2}}(x,y,z) = \frac{1}{9\sqrt{30\pi}} \left(3z^2 - r^2\right) e^{-r/3}$$
$$\psi_{3d_{xy}}(x,y,z) = \frac{1}{9\sqrt{30\pi}} xy \cdot e^{-r/3}$$

The visualization renders a **2D cross-section** of the orbital density in the $xz$-plane ($y = 0$) using an HTML Canvas and D3.


### 2. Wavefunction evaluation per pixel
For each $(x_i, z_j)$:
- Compute $r = \sqrt{x^2 + z^2}$, $\theta = \arccos(z/r)$, $\phi = \operatorname{atan2}(0, x)$
- Evaluate the radial part $R_{n,l}(r)$ via the generalised Laguerre polynomial $L_{n-l-1}^{2l+1}$
- Evaluate $|Y_l^m(\theta, \phi)|$ via the associated Legendre polynomial $P_l^m$
- Store $|\psi| = R_{n,l} \cdot |Y_l^m|$ (note: **amplitude**, not probability density $|\psi|^2$)

### 3. Colour mapping
- Find $\max|\psi|$ over the grid for normalisation
- Apply the **Magma** perceptually-uniform colormap (D3): black $\to$ purple $\to$ orange $\to$ yellow represents low $\to$ high amplitude
- Write RGB triplets directly into a `Canvas ImageData` buffer for O(1) per-pixel cost

### 4. Interactive controls
- React `useState` tracks $(n, l, m)$; any change triggers a full re-render
- Cascading validation keeps $l < n$ and $|m| \le l$ automatically

## Interpreting the orbital plot

Each image is a **heat-map of the wavefunction amplitude** $|\psi_{n,l,m}(x, 0, z)|$ on the $y = 0$ cross-section, rendered with the **Magma** colormap:

| Colour | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| Black / dark purple | Near-zero amplitude — the electron is essentially never found here |
| Orange / bright yellow | High amplitude — most probable region for the electron |

**What to look for when changing quantum numbers:**

- **Increasing $n$** makes the orbital larger and adds more radial nodes (dark concentric rings). The number of radial nodes equals $n - l - 1$.
- **Increasing $l$** at fixed $n$ removes radial nodes and adds angular nodes (angular dark bands). An $s$-orbital ($l=0$) is spherically symmetric; a $p$-orbital ($l=1$) has a nodal plane; a $d$-orbital ($l=2$) has two nodal surfaces.
- **Increasing $|m|$** at fixed $l$ rotates the angular distribution, concentrating density away from the $z$-axis (the cross-section becomes more ring-like).

> The plot shows $|\psi|$ rather than $|\psi|^2$: the amplitude emphasises the overall lobe structure while the probability density $|\psi|^2$ would sharpen the bright regions and further suppress the dark ones.
